Memory game

Three habits that prevent harm.

Tap a card when you're ready to test what stuck. No passive scrolling here.

01The Rights

Check every one.

Tap to reveal →
The Rights

Right patient, drug, dose, route, time — plus right documentation, reason, and response. Skip one and you've opened the door to harm.

02High-alert

Slow down for these.

Tap to reveal →
High-alert

Insulin, heparin/anticoagulants, opioids, and concentrated electrolytes (like IV potassium) cause the most serious errors. Many need an independent double-check.

03Two IDs

Every time.

Tap to reveal →
Two IDs

Two identifiers (name + date of birth), and scan the band. "I know which are mine" is never an identifier.

Chart test

Read the INR trend.

Chart & trend test

A patient on warfarin. Their INR over three days. What's your action?

DayINRNote
Mon2.1Therapeutic
Wed3.4Rising
Fri5.8Gums bleeding
Why:

An INR of 5.8 with bleeding is dangerously high — hold the warfarin, notify the provider, and anticipate vitamin K. The trend and the bleeding both scream stop.

Clinical quick check

Now — make the call.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 1 of 5

Before giving a medication, how many patient identifiers are required?

Why:

Always two identifiers (name + DOB), and scan the band.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 2 of 5

Which is considered a high-alert medication?

Why:

Insulin, heparin, opioids, and concentrated electrolytes are high-alert — slow down and double-check.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 3 of 5

The heart rate is 46 before a dose of metoprolol. The nurse should:

Why:

Beta-blockers are held for bradycardia — hold and notify when the HR is below the parameter.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 4 of 5

A capsule is labeled extended-release. The patient has a feeding tube. The nurse should:

Why:

Crushing extended-release drugs can cause a dangerous dose dump — ask pharmacy for an appropriate formulation.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 5 of 5

Two patients share a last name. The safest action is to:

Why:

Same-name patients are a classic error trap — two identifiers and the band scan every time.

Next Gen NCLEX · SATA

Which of these are high-alert medications? Select all that apply.

Why:

Insulin, heparin, concentrated electrolytes, and opioids are high-alert. A stool softener is low-risk.
