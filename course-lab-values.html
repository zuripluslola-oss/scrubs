The quick recall

Three things to carry with you.

A number is a story.

Recognize

K⁺ 6.8 is hyperkalemia (normal 3.5–5.0). The body's warnings: muscle weakness, palpitations, and on the monitor — peaked T waves.

02Prioritize

Protect the heart first.

Tap to reveal →
Prioritize

Cardiac stability beats everything. Monitor + 12-lead ECG now. High potassium kills through the heart, not the lab slip.

03Act

Stop, check, escalate.

Tap to reveal →
Act

Hold potassium sources, notify the provider (SBAR), and anticipate: calcium to protect, insulin + D50 to shift, kayexalate/dialysis to remove.

Confidence check

How sure are you on the critical potassium value?

Not sureLocked in

Normal K⁺ is 3.5–5.0 mEq/L — a banana costs $3.50 to $5.00. Above 6.0 is critical: peaked T's, weakness, “protect the heart.” Below 2.5 is critical too — cramps and arrhythmias. Rating your own certainty before the reveal is what makes it stick.

Clinical quick check

Now — make the call.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 1 of 3

Your patient has a potassium of 6.8, new palpitations, and sudden weakness. What is your priority?

Choose the safest next step.
Why:

Hyperkalemia is dangerous because of its effect on the heart. Monitoring and an ECG come first — peaked T waves and lethal arrhythmias can develop fast, long before documentation matters.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 2 of 3

The provider orders IV calcium gluconate. What is its role in hyperkalemia?

Choose the safest next step.
Why:

Calcium gluconate doesn't lower potassium — it protects the heart by stabilizing the cardiac membrane, buying time while insulin + D50 shift it and kayexalate or dialysis remove it.

Clinical quick checkQuestion 3 of 3

Which finding would you expect on the ECG of a patient with a potassium of 6.8?

Choose the safest next step.
Why:

Peaked, tented T waves are the classic early sign of high potassium. U waves point the other way — toward low potassium. Build the pattern: high K⁺ → peaked T's.
